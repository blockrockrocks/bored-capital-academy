# Module 1 — Why NFT prices are misleading

## The floor-price illusion
Ask vs. bid vs. completed sale. The floor is the cheapest live *listing*: an ask, not a price anyone paid.
The best bid is the highest live offer: what one token could be sold for right now (if the bid is funded).
A completed sale is historical evidence, and its relevance decays with time and depends on the token sold.
Quoted market cap (floor × supply) is a headline measure, not the cash that owners could collectively
realise. Liquidity, comparable-sale quality, bid depth and selling pressure matter.

## Real liquidity
Listing depth (how many tokens are listed near the floor), offers (collection bids and their quantity),
trade size, order-book gaps and slippage. The exit curve asks: if I sold N tokens into the current bid
book, best bid first, what average price would I receive and how far below the floor is it?

## Trading quality
Volume, turnover (distinct tokens traded / supply), repeat trading, marketplace incentives and wash-trade
red flags. Red flags include self-trades, reciprocal trading between the same wallets, and the same token
traded repeatedly in a short window. A flag is a prompt to investigate, not proof.

## Ownership structure
Wallet concentration (top-10 share, wallets controlling 50%, HHI), listed float, common control and
uncertainty. Wallets are not people: one person can control many wallets, and tokens held by contracts
(vaults, escrow, lending, multisigs) hide the real economic owner.

## Example exercise: the liquidity gap
Compare three observations for the same collection: the lowest listing, the highest actionable
collection offer and the latest completed comparable sales. Discuss why these do not imply the same
exit price.

## Research hygiene
Date every data point. Cross-check sales against collection authenticity and duplicate listings.
Label assumptions and unavailable data instead of filling gaps with guesses.
